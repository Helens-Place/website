Most families who ring me have already done some homework. They have often been quoted for an online assessment somewhere, seen that it can be quicker and sometimes cheaper, and they want to know why I work the way I do. It is a fair question, and I would rather answer it plainly than pretend the choice is not there.

So here is how I run an assessment, and why. None of it is about being precious. It comes down to what a diagnostic assessment needs if it is going to be worth the money and worth the day.

## Why I do it in person

An assessment is not just a stack of scores. A good deal of what tells me how a young person learns happens in the room, in the small moments around the tasks rather than in the tasks themselves.

I am watching what happens when something gets hard. Does the child go quiet, start fidgeting, crack a joke to change the subject, glance across at their parent, or simply put the pencil down and decide they are rubbish at it? Do their shoulders drop when they see a page of text? Are they slower because the reading is hard, or because they are anxious, or because it is nearly lunch and they have had enough? Those things matter, and they change how I read the numbers.

You lose most of that through a screen. A camera flattens a child into a small square, and you cannot see the jiggling feet, the held breath, or the quiet decision to give up that a child will hide if they possibly can. My work has consistently found that good support for dyslexic learners runs on relationships and on people who actually notice what is going on, from my research on [teachers' agency and the relationships around dyslexia support (Ross, 2017)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8578.12174) to a [case study on building a safe space for vulnerable young people (Ross, 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9604.12246), where the whole point was that support works best when it is proactive rather than patched on after the fact. It is hard to be proactive about something you never saw.

## One young person a day, and as long as it takes

I set aside four to five hours for an assessment, and I only ever see one young person in a day. There is nobody in the waiting room after them, so we are never watching the clock.

The actual contact time varies. If we are looking at numeracy as well as literacy it runs longer, and a fair bit of the day is breaks, biscuits and talking rather than testing. That is deliberate. It is an intense process for a young person, and I chunk everything down. I will not put two or three reading tasks back to back, because doing that when reading is the very thing you find hard is miserable and it tells me very little. For a younger child I will often split the work across two shorter sessions rather than push them through in one sitting. There is more on the shape of the day in [what to expect](/what-to-expect).

The reason I leave so much time is simple. The assessment can then take as long as it honestly needs. I would far rather run over and get a true picture than rush a child and hand you a tidy report that does not quite fit them.

## The people in the room, not just the child

An assessment involves the adult who comes along, usually a parent, as well as the young person. I want your account of how things look at home, what the early years were like, what school has said, and the thing that worries you at two in the morning. That history shapes what I am seeing on the day, and it is far easier to talk through properly when we are sitting in the same room.

I also always make space for the young person's own view of themselves, because it gets left out far too often and it matters enormously. My study [*'I'm Dyslexic but What Does That Even Mean?'* (Ross, 2021)](https://doi.org/10.16993/sjdr.782) is built entirely around how a young person's understanding of their own dyslexia shapes whether they take up the help on offer. So I talk with them, properly, rather than at them.

## A report that anyone can read

You leave with a full written report, and for me that is two weeks maximum after the day. It is a long document, because it has to be. It meets every standard my registration and Assessment Practising Certificate require, which is what makes it hold up for schools, exam boards, universities and employers.

I also write it so that a human being can actually read it. A report is no use to anyone if it is a wall of jargon and standardised scores that a parent cannot decode and a busy teacher has no time to unpick. Mine says plainly whether the profile is consistent with dyslexia, what this particular young person is genuinely good at, and what school can do about it on Monday morning. I do that on purpose, because my research on how families engage with support found that getting help too often feels like a [battle (Ross, 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8578.12254), and a clear report is one of the best ways I know of taking the heat out of those conversations.

## Why there is usually a wait

I will be honest about this. I am generally booked around three months ahead. I know that is hard to hear when you are worried about your child now and you would happily have it sorted yesterday.

That wait is the plain consequence of working the way I have just described. If I see one family a day and I let each assessment take the time it needs, I cannot get through as many people as somewhere running several in a day, or doing them online. I have made my peace with that, because I am not willing to trade the quality of the day for the length of the diary. If the timing is difficult, book a [free 20-minute call](/contact) anyway. Often I can point you to something useful in the meantime, and it means you are ready to go the moment a date comes up.

## Questions people often ask

**Do you offer online dyslexia assessments?**
No. I assess in person, because so much of what I need to see happens in the room: how a young person reacts when a task gets hard, their body language, whether they are anxious, tired, or simply finding the reading difficult. A screen hides most of that. I would rather you waited a little longer for an in-person appointment than had a thinner assessment sooner.

**Why do you only see one young person a day?**
Because an assessment should take as long as it needs, and that is impossible if someone else is booked in behind. One family a day means we can take proper breaks, follow something up when it turns out to matter, and never rush a tired child.

**How long will I wait for an appointment?**
I am usually booked around three months ahead. That is the honest cost of not rushing and only assessing one young person a day. Book a free call and I will tell you where things stand and what I would suggest in the meantime.

**Does a parent need to be there?**
Yes, for part of it at least. Your account of the history and of daily life at home is a real part of the picture, and I will want to talk it through with you as well as working with your child.

**Can the assessment be split over more than one day?**
Yes, especially for younger children. Some do far better in two shorter sessions than one long one, and we will do whatever suits your child best.
