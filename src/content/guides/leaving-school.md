## Why girls in particular

Public debate tends to swing between claims that neurodiversity is over-diagnosed
and evidence pointing the other way, such as the underdiagnosis of ADHD in girls,
or the finding that most dyslexic people leave school without a formal diagnosis.

Underneath that argument is the thing that actually matters. Girls fare
particularly poorly here. Without help, many risk becoming a shell of what they
could be, when the right support would have built their confidence and given them
the skills to advocate and care for themselves.

Part of the reason is that difficulties in girls are more often masked, and
screening does not always account for that. What gets noticed is the effect on
attainment and wellbeing, not the cause.

## What institutions need to do

The other half of the finding is not about young people at all. Nobody should be
expected to navigate an inaccessible system independently, however good their
self-advocacy.

Schools, colleges, employers and other institutions have to create environments
where differences are understood and where asking for support leads to something
actually happening. Responsibility is shared: young people should be supported to
advocate, and the systems around them must be willing and able to listen and
respond.

Several of the changes that make the most difference cost nothing:

- **Warm referrals.** Introduce someone to the next person rather than pointing
  them at a webpage.
- **A clear menu of adjustments** staff can make quickly, so support is not
  delayed while evidence is gathered.
- **Quiet spaces and an agreed, non-punitive way** to step away, regulate and
  come back.
- **Explicit expectations.** Deadlines, conventions and unwritten rules said out
  loud rather than assumed.
- **Basic sensory review** of lighting, noise, temperature and crowding.

## Where this comes from

This guide draws on my Churchill Fellowship, which looked at how neurodivergent
girls and young women aged 16 to 25 can be better supported as they move beyond
compulsory schooling. I spent 2026 visiting education, training and support
settings in Victoria and Tasmania in Australia, and in Texas and Florida in the
United States.

The full report makes 30 recommendations across policy, institutions and
immediate practice, and is free to download.
